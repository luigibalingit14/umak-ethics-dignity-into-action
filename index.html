1. Question and bounded case

This project asks: How can humility and receptivity coexist with agency and courage when a university student is pressured by peers to stay silent about a classmate's contribution in a group project? The illustrative case is fictional: a teammate offers a source and an idea; the group dismisses them without checking and urges “Javier” to stay quiet so the team can finish. No real student, UMak incident, campus policy, or prevalence claim is represented.

2. Method and source selection

We use qualitative documentary analysis: close-read the assigned passages, record each author's claim and page, then compare the texts' concepts with a fictional decision. Four anchor entries come from von Hildebrand's Parts V–VI (supplied PDF pp. 31–32 and 37) and Gray and Martin's Chapter 4 (PDF pp. 40–41). Three supplementary sources serve different purposes: the Catechism supplies Catholic normative teaching; Asch's controlled conformity study supplies limited behavioral context; and Philippine Republic Act No. 8293 supplies a narrow local legal context on copyright and attribution. These are not interchangeable kinds of evidence. We conducted no survey, interview, or observation, so we cannot infer how often this occurs at UMak or how students would respond.

For each matrix entry, we record the source type and location, summarize its claim, explain its relevance, and state a limitation. The comparison is interpretive, not a causal test: theology, a laboratory study, and a statute cannot independently confirm one another. Asch's line-judgment task may help explain why unanimity can feel influential, but it differs from a dispute about credit. We have no Philippine student data on this scenario; that contextual gap remains and should not be filled with assumptions.

Each interpretation follows a three-step check: locate the assigned passage, paraphrase its claim in its original context, and label the move from text to scenario as our application. We then compare the recommendation with the objection and source limitations. This keeps authorial claims, external evidence, and our ethical judgment distinct; it does not replace member review or instructor approval.

3. Reading the virtues together

In Part V, von Hildebrand presents Christ's freely accepted weakness as a lesson in humility (PDF pp. 31–32). Our application is to acknowledge uncertainty, check the source, and avoid treating the group—or Javier—as automatically right. In Part VI, she describes receptivity within a specifically religious and maternal account of women's vocation (PDF p. 37). We cautiously use listening and examining a contribution as an analogy; the passage does not define general empathy or directly address group projects. That difference in context is a real interpretive limitation, not something the case can erase.

Gray and Martin discuss fortitude in Chapter 4 as firmness in pursuing the good despite difficulty and fear (PDF pp. 40–41). Here, agency is our working label for Javier's responsibility to decide and act, not a virtue named in those passages. Their common Chapter 1 reading discusses acting and cooperating with grace (PDF p. 14), but it does not supply a tested procedure for resolving team disputes. Read together, the texts suggest a possible sequence—not a guarantee: receptivity and humility can inform judgment; agency selects a response; fortitude can support a measured action. Catholic teaching on prudence and fortitude (Catechism, §§1806, 1808) helps distinguish courageous speech from rash confrontation. Asch (1956, pp. 1–70) studied conformity in controlled line judgments; that historical laboratory task does not establish what students do in real ethical conflicts.

4. Serious objection and response

A fair objection is that the group may not be suppressing a classmate's work at all: an idea may be unverified, independently developed, or difficult to attribute in a genuinely collaborative report. Philippine copyright law does not protect ideas as such (RA 8293, §175), and §196 says attribution of a contribution to a collective work is deemed waived unless expressly reserved. Copyright rules therefore do not prove that the fictional teammate has a legal claim or that the group acted improperly. The response is ethical and procedural, not a legal accusation: first check what the teammate actually contributed, the source, any prior agreement, and the course's stated credit rules. A respectful request for accurate acknowledgment can still be reasonable, but neither a student's silence nor a group's decision settles the facts by itself.

5. Proportionate recommendations

  1. Listen and verify: Ask the teammate to explain the source and contribution; review it before deciding. This protects against both dismissing valid work and crediting unsupported claims.
  2. Raise a precise concern: “Can we check this source and agree how to acknowledge each person's contribution?” Address the work, not anyone's character. Choose a private or public setting based on safety and what is likely to help; courage does not require humiliation or confrontation at any cost.
  3. Use a proportionate next step: If unresolved, keep a factual, minimal record and ask the instructor through the class's normal channel how to apply the course rules. Do not circulate private messages or allege a policy or legal violation without evidence.

These are reasoned options for a constructed case, not findings about UMak or rules that fit every situation. The evidence matrix gives each source's exact location, relevance, and limitation; the student-facing webpage turns the recommendations into a short output.

Our application: Listen to the teammate and examine their offered source before judging or dismissing the idea.

Limit: The passage is about religious vocation and motherhood, not general empathy or group-project listening. Applying it here is an analogy, not the author's stated conclusion.

Principal Book 2 Emmaus Road

Boys to Men: The Transforming Power of Virtue

by Tim Gray & Curtis Martin

Anchor 3 • Fortitude • Chapter 4, PDF p. 40

Authors' idea: Gray and Martin describe fortitude as courage that holds the will to what reason identifies as good despite difficulty and fear.

Our application: Javier can ask the group to review the source and acknowledge the teammate's contribution even if friends want to move on.

Limit: The chapter develops a Christian virtue account; it does not measure student behavior or say that public confrontation is always best.

Anchor 4 • Courage in action • Chapter 4, PDF p. 41

Authors' idea: The authors contrast fear-driven retreat into spectatorship with courage that helps a person undertake difficult work and persevere.

Our application: Courage can mean a calm, specific request for fair review—not an insult, a threat, or a demand that peers agree without checking evidence.

Limit: The case still needs prudence: the text does not establish how peers will react or require a student to take an unsafe risk.

A Careful Working Synthesis

Our interpretation: Humility and receptivity can shape how a student listens and checks their assumptions. Agency and fortitude can support a responsible response. This synthesis is our application; neither book analyzes the fictional group-project case.
Tension to examine: The ideas do not automatically resolve every case. Receptivity is not passive agreement, humility is not self-erasure, agency is not acting alone, and courage is not the same as confrontation. Prudence helps select a proportionate next step; silence may also reflect uncertainty, a power imbalance, or a genuine safety concern.

A Proportionate Speaking-Up Plan

Options, not a one-size-fits-all rule

Use these steps to weigh a decision, not to assume every disagreement is bullying or that public confrontation is always required.

1

1. Listen and check the source

Ask the teammate to explain the idea and source. Review the evidence before deciding whether it belongs in the report. Humility means being willing to correct your own first impression.

Virtues in tension: Receptivity does not require agreeing. Listening and checking can reveal that either the teammate or the group has misunderstood something.
2

2. Raise a specific, respectful concern

Try: "Can we check this source before leaving the idea out, and make sure the report credits everyone's contribution?" Explain the concern without attacking a teammate's character.

Virtues in tension: Agency means choosing a response; courage/fortitude can help persist through discomfort. Neither guarantees the group will agree.
3

3. Choose a proportionate next step

If the concern is still dismissed, record the relevant project facts and ask the instructor through the class's normal channel how to handle contribution or credit. Consider a private conversation if it is safe and likely to help.

Limit: This project has not verified any specific UMak office, reporting procedure, or outcome. Follow actual course guidance; do not escalate in a way that creates avoidable risk.
