Dignity, Courage, into Action: Brief & Ethical Judgment

Group 3 • Speaking up under peer pressure

1. Context and Core Question

This project examines a common ethical decision in university life: how to respond when friends pressure a student to keep quiet rather than address a classmate's treatment fairly. The case below is fictional and illustrative; it is not a report of an incident at UMak, evidence of how common peer pressure is, or a claim about a university policy.

Research Question: "How can humility and receptivity coexist with agency and courage when a university student is pressured by peers to stay silent about a classmate's contribution in a group project?"

Fictional college-life case: During a group-project meeting, a teammate offers a source and an idea for the report. The group dismisses the contribution without checking the source, then pressures "Javier" to stay quiet so the team can finish quickly. Javier must decide whether to let the omission stand, challenge the group, or raise the concern in a respectful and proportionate way. No real student or event is represented.

2. Theoretical Framework (Analysis Using the Four Core Virtues)

The project reads the assigned passages closely. Humility and receptivity are discussed in von Hildebrand's Christian account of women's vocation; courage/fortitude is discussed by Gray and Martin. "Agency" is our working term for the student's responsibility to choose and act; it is not presented as a named virtue in the cited Chapter 4 passages.

A. From The Privilege of Being a Woman by Alice von Hildebrand:
These passages are theological arguments about the Incarnation, humility, and women's religious vocation—not empirical findings about all women or direct advice about university group work. We apply them to the fictional case cautiously and name that interpretive step.

  • Humility (our application): In Part V, von Hildebrand presents Christ's freely accepted weakness as a lesson in humility. Applied here, humility means not assuming the group—or Javier—is automatically right: check the offered source, acknowledge uncertainty, and avoid using "speaking up" to dominate or shame others.
  • Receptivity (our application): In Part VI, von Hildebrand presents receptivity through a specifically religious and maternal account of women's vocation. We cautiously apply the idea of being open to what is received: listen to the teammate and examine their contribution before judging it. The passage does not itself define receptivity as general empathy or discuss group projects.

B. From Boys to Men: The Transforming Power of Virtue by Tim Gray and Curtis Martin:
Chapter 4 examines fortitude as courage in the face of difficulty and fear. The group's common reading, Chapter 1, also discusses the need to act and cooperate with grace (PDF p. 14); we use "agency" as our label for the decision-making application rather than attributing that term to the authors.

  • Agency (our working term): Javier must decide whether to check the source, acknowledge the teammate's contribution, and raise the concern. The guide's common Chapter 1 reading (PDF p. 14) supports deliberate moral effort; "agency" is our analytical label, not a direct quotation or named Chapter 4 virtue.
  • Courage / Fortitude: In Chapter 4, Gray and Martin describe fortitude as steadiness in pursuing the good despite difficulty and fear (PDF pp. 40–41). Applied here, courage may mean asking the group to review the source and credit the teammate—calmly, not by humiliating peers or taking an unsafe risk.

3. Synthesis of the Books (Connection & Tension)

Working synthesis, not a direct claim by either author: Listening carefully and admitting our limits can shape how we act; responsibility and courage can turn concern into a respectful request for fairness.

In this case, receptivity is our analogy for hearing and examining the teammate's contribution; agency is our term for choosing a response, and fortitude may support a difficult but measured request. These ideas do not determine the facts, make silence inherently wrong, or guarantee an outcome.

4. What the supplementary evidence can—and cannot—show

A controlled conformity study, a college bystander-program evaluation, and Catholic moral teaching contribute different kinds of evidence. The studies do not establish how common peer pressure is at UMak or prove what Javier must do; the Catechism offers a Catholic moral framework, not behavioral data. See the evidence matrix for each source's method, relevance, and limits.

5. A proportionate response to the fictional case

The following are reasoned options for this constructed scenario, not a claim that one response fits every situation:

  1. Listen and check (receptivity and humility): Ask the teammate to explain the source and idea; review it before deciding whether it belongs in the report.
  2. Raise a specific, respectful concern (agency and courage): For example: "Can we check this source before we leave it out, and make sure the report credits everyone's contribution?" Address the decision, not a peer's character.
  3. Choose a proportionate next step: If the group still dismisses the concern, keep a factual record of the project discussion and ask the instructor through the class's normal channel how to handle contribution or credit disputes. Do not invent a campus office, policy, or guaranteed outcome.

A Careful Working Synthesis

Our interpretation: Humility and receptivity can shape how a student listens and checks their assumptions. Agency and fortitude can support a responsible response. This synthesis is our application; neither book analyzes the fictional group-project case.
Tension to examine: The ideas do not automatically resolve every case. Receptivity is not passive agreement, humility is not self-erasure, agency is not acting alone, and courage is not the same as confrontation. Prudence helps select a proportionate next step; silence may also reflect uncertainty, a power imbalance, or a genuine safety concern.

A Proportionate Speaking-Up Plan

Options, not a one-size-fits-all rule

Use these steps to weigh a decision, not to assume every disagreement is bullying or that public confrontation is always required.

1

1. Listen and check the source

Ask the teammate to explain the idea and source. Review the evidence before deciding whether it belongs in the report. Humility means being willing to correct your own first impression.

Virtues in tension: Receptivity does not require agreeing. Listening and checking can reveal that either the teammate or the group has misunderstood something.
2

2. Raise a specific, respectful concern

Try: "Can we check this source before leaving the idea out, and make sure the report credits everyone's contribution?" Explain the concern without attacking a teammate's character.

Virtues in tension: Agency means choosing a response; courage/fortitude can help persist through discomfort. Neither guarantees the group will agree.
3

3. Choose a proportionate next step

If the concern is still dismissed, record the relevant project facts and ask the instructor through the class's normal channel how to handle contribution or credit. Consider a private conversation if it is safe and likely to help.

Limit: This project has not verified any specific UMak office, reporting procedure, or outcome. Follow actual course guidance; do not escalate in a way that creates avoidable risk.
