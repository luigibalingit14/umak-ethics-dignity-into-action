1. Question and bounded case

This project asks: How can humility and receptivity coexist with agency and courage when a university student is pressured by peers to stay silent about a classmate's contribution in a group project? The illustrative case is fictional: a teammate offers a source and an idea; the group dismisses them without checking and urges "Javier" to stay quiet so the team can finish. Three parties are affected: the teammate whose work may go uncredited, the group whose report may misstate its sources, and Javier, who must choose between comfort and accuracy. Our audience is university students doing graded group work. No real student, UMak incident, campus policy, or prevalence claim is represented.

2. Method and source selection

We use qualitative documentary analysis: locate each assigned passage, paraphrase the author's claim in its original context, then label our move to the fictional case as an application. Anchors come from von Hildebrand's Parts V–VI and Gray and Martin's Chapter 4; three supplementary sources supply Catholic normative teaching, behavioural context, and Philippine legal context. These are different kinds of evidence and cannot confirm one another. We conducted no survey, interview, or observation, so we cannot infer prevalence at UMak or how students would respond.

3. Reading the virtues together

Von Hildebrand's Part V presents Christ's freely accepted weakness as a lesson in humility: real strength is acknowledging that we cannot suffice on our own (von Hildebrand, 2002, Part V, PDF pp. 30–32). Part II states that men and women are "perfectly equal in dignity," both made to God's image (Part II, PDF pp. 10–14); this is our dignity premise, not merely a call to be fair. Part VI distinguishes receptivity from Aristotelian passivity, describing an "alert, awakened, joyful readiness," and states that all created persons are essentially receptive (Part VI, PDF pp. 35–37). That sentence is our warrant for applying receptivity to a male student: the author universalises it to creatures, even while illustrating it through Mary's assent and maternity. Her wider account remains gendered and theological; we borrow the structure of receiving, not her claims about women's nature.

Gray and Martin define fortitude as binding the will to the good of reason despite fear (2001, ch. 4, PDF pp. 39–40). Their own examples are social: most of us fear to say no to others because we want to be liked, and Peter's denial, where he "fails to be both courageous and just" (ch. 4, PDF pp. 41–42). This links our strand to justice: accurate credit is what a teammate is due. The connection is complementarity, not sequence. Chapter 1's sailboat makes the sails our effort and the wind God's grace (ch. 1, PDF pp. 14–15 ); Chapter 9 insists the virtues are unified and end in friendship (ch. 9, PDF pp. 81–82). Receptivity receives; agency acts; fortitude sustains. Agency is our working label, not a named virtue. The tension: humility in Part V is dependence on grace, not merely open-mindedness, so our use of it remains an analogy.

4. Contemporary evidence and objection

A serious objection is that no one is being suppressed: the idea may be unverified, independently developed, or hard to attribute in genuinely shared work. Asch's conformity experiments (1956) are widely reported as showing that a unanimous majority can shift an individual's judgment. We reached this only through secondary summaries and did not open the 1956 monograph, so we treat it as background, not as a finding about our case; it also studied perceptual judgments, not credit disputes among friends.

Philippine law does not protect ideas as such (Republic Act No. 8293, 1997, §175). But §196's deemed waiver applies only to a 'collective work' as defined in §171.2 (issued under another's name with contributors unidentified). A named group report is joint authorship (§178.2), where attribution is a moral right (§193.1). Our claim is therefore ethical and procedural, not legal: verify the contribution, the source, prior agreements, and the course's stated credit rules.

5. Judgment and action

Option C is most defensible. Staying silent (A) preserves comfort but leaves a possibly valid contribution uncredited and a source unchecked; accusing the group publicly (B) asserts a conclusion before the facts are known and risks humiliating people who may be mistaken rather than dishonest. Neither humility nor courage requires either extreme.

  1. Listen and verify: ask the teammate to explain the source and contribution and review it. This guards against dismissing valid work and against crediting unsupported claims.
  2. Raise a precise concern: "Can we check this source and agree how to acknowledge each person's contribution?" Address the work, not character; choose a private or group setting according to safety and likely effect (Catholic Church, 1997, sec. 1806).
  3. Take a proportionate next step: if unresolved, keep a minimal factual record and ask the instructor through the class's normal channel how to apply course rules.

Safeguards: do not circulate private messages, do not allege legal or policy violations without evidence, and do not treat silence as cowardice, since it may reflect uncertainty, unequal power, or real risk. These are reasoned options for a constructed case, not findings about UMak.

Four Anchor Passages and Their Application

2 passages from each book

PDF page numbers below use the supplied PDF viewer's page counter. The author summaries, our applications, and the limitations are identified separately. The Strand 3 focal readings do not directly discuss this fictional college case.

Principal Book 1 Sapientia Press

The Privilege of Being a Woman

by Alice von Hildebrand

Anchor 1 • Humility • Part V, PDF pp. 30–32

Author's idea: In her account of the Incarnation, von Hildebrand presents Christ's freely accepted weakness as a lesson in humility; she also describes acknowledging one's weakness rather than claiming self-sufficiency. She adds that true strength is knowing how weak one is, because that awareness calls for help.

Our application: Javier should not assume that speaking louder makes him right. He can admit uncertainty, check the teammate's source, and raise the credit concern without belittling anyone.

Limit: This is a Christian theological argument, not a study of peer pressure or a universal psychological description.

Anchor 2 • Receptivity • Part VI, PDF pp. 35–37

Author's idea: At the end of Part VI, von Hildebrand describes receptivity through a specifically religious and maternal account of women's vocation, using Mary's assent as an example. Part VI explicitly rejects Aristotle's equation of the female with passivity, and states that "all created persons are essentially receptive."

Our application: Listen to the teammate and examine their offered source before judging or dismissing the idea. This is our textual warrant for applying receptivity to Javier; the maternal and religious framing remains the author's, not ours.

Limit: The passage is about religious vocation and motherhood, not general empathy or group-project listening. Applying it here is an analogy, not the author's stated conclusion.

Principal Book 2 Emmaus Road

Boys to Men: The Transforming Power of Virtue

by Tim Gray & Curtis Martin

Anchor 3 • Fortitude • Chapter 4, PDF pp. 39–40

Authors' idea: Gray and Martin describe fortitude as courage that holds the will to what reason identifies as good despite difficulty and fear.

Our application: Javier can ask the group to review the source and acknowledge the teammate's contribution even if friends want to move on.

Limit: The chapter develops a Christian virtue account; it does not measure student behavior or say that public confrontation is always best.

Anchor 4 • Courage in action • Chapter 4, PDF pp. 41–42

Authors' idea: The authors contrast fear-driven retreat into spectatorship with courage that helps a person undertake difficult work and persevere. The same chapter notes that most of us fear to say no because we want to be liked, and that Peter's denial "fails to be both courageous and just."

Our application: Courage can mean a calm, specific request for fair review—not an insult, a threat, or a demand that peers agree without checking evidence.

Limit: The case still needs prudence: the text does not establish how peers will react or require a student to take an unsafe risk.

A Careful Working Synthesis

Our interpretation: Humility and receptivity can shape how a student listens and checks their assumptions. Agency and fortitude can support a responsible response. This synthesis is our application; neither book analyzes the fictional group-project case.
Tension to examine: The ideas do not automatically resolve every case. Receptivity is not passive agreement, humility is not self-erasure, agency is not acting alone, and courage is not the same as confrontation. Prudence helps select a proportionate next step; silence may also reflect uncertainty, a power imbalance, or a genuine safety concern.

Main idea: Fortitude holds the will firmly to what reason identifies as good in the face of difficulty; fear can prevent a person from acting.

Connection: If the source supports the teammate's point, Javier can choose to ask that the team review and credit it despite pressure to stay quiet.

Question / limitation: What is the good action when facts are uncertain? Fortitude does not replace checking evidence or deciding how to speak prudently.

Working term and common reading

Agency: Our analytical label for the student's responsibility to choose and act. Gray and Martin's assigned common reading, Chapter 1, “Men and Virtue,” PDF p. 14, discusses acting and cooperating with grace; it does not define a virtue called “agency.” Chapter 1 (PDF pp. 14–15 ) uses a sailboat: the sails are our effort, the wind is God's grace. Chapter 9 (PDF pp. 81–82) argues the virtues are unified and that their goal is friendship. We use these to frame receptivity and agency as complementary, not competing.

Reasoned application to the fictional case

A Proportionate Speaking-Up Plan

Options, not a one-size-fits-all rule

Use these steps to weigh a decision, not to assume every disagreement is bullying or that public confrontation is always required.

1

1. Listen and check the source

Ask the teammate to explain the idea and source. Review the evidence before deciding whether it belongs in the report. Humility means being willing to correct your own first impression.

Virtues in tension: Receptivity does not require agreeing. Listening and checking can reveal that either the teammate or the group has misunderstood something.
2

2. Raise a specific, respectful concern

Try: "Can we check this source before leaving the idea out, and make sure the report credits everyone's contribution?" Explain the concern without attacking a teammate's character.

Virtues in tension: Agency means choosing a response; courage/fortitude can help persist through discomfort. Neither guarantees the group will agree.
3

3. Choose a proportionate next step

If the concern is still dismissed, record the relevant project facts and ask the instructor through the class's normal channel how to handle contribution or credit. Consider a private conversation if it is safe and likely to help.

Limit: This project has not verified any specific UMak office, reporting procedure, or outcome. Follow actual course guidance; do not escalate in a way that creates avoidable risk.
