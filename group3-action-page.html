Listen, Check, Speak

Speaking up under peer pressure without losing humility or receptivity

Research question: How can humility and receptivity coexist with agency and courage when a university student is pressured by peers to stay silent about a classmate's contribution in a group project?

A fictional college-life case

In a group-project meeting, a teammate offers a source and an idea for the report. The group dismisses them without checking and urges “Javier” to stay quiet so everyone can finish. Javier must decide whether to ignore the concern, challenge the group, or ask for a fair review. The case is fictional, not evidence about UMak.

How the virtues can work together

In Part V of The Privilege of Being a Woman, Alice von Hildebrand presents Christ's freely accepted weakness as a lesson in humility (supplied PDF pp. 31–32). We apply humility as a reason to admit uncertainty, check evidence, and avoid assuming either Javier or the group is automatically right. In Part VI, she describes receptivity within a specifically religious and maternal account of women's vocation (PDF p. 37). Applying it to listening to a classmate is our analogy, not the passage's definition of empathy or group-work advice.

Tim Gray and Curtis Martin discuss fortitude in Chapter 4 of Boys to Men: The Transforming Power of Virtue as firmness in pursuing the good despite difficulty and fear (PDF pp. 40–41). We use agency as our working term for Javier's responsibility to choose and act; it is not a virtue named in those passages. Humility and receptivity can shape how he judges the claim; agency chooses what to do; fortitude may help him take a measured next step. None guarantees that his interpretation is correct or that the group will agree.

Important tension: Receptivity does not mean automatic agreement or passivity. A person can listen carefully, test a claim, and still disagree. Courage does not require public confrontation at any cost; good judgment includes timing, safety, and the possibility that the facts may change the conclusion.

A serious objection

Perhaps the group is not suppressing anyone's work: the source may not support the idea, contributions may overlap, or the team may have no agreed credit process. Philippine Republic Act No. 8293 excludes ideas as such from copyright protection (§175), and §196 addresses attribution in collective works. These rules do not prove wrongdoing or settle the facts. Verify the contribution, source, prior agreements, and course rules before making a legal accusation. A courteous request for acknowledgment can still be reasonable even when copyright law does not resolve the question.

Three proportionate steps

  1. Listen and check. Ask the teammate to explain the source and contribution; review it before deciding. This avoids dismissing valid work or crediting unsupported claims.
  2. Raise a specific concern. Try: “Can we check this source and agree how to acknowledge each person's contribution?” Address the decision, not a teammate's character. Choose a private or group setting based on safety and what is likely to help.
  3. Use a fair next step. If unresolved, keep a factual, minimal record and ask the instructor through the class's normal channel how to apply course rules. Do not share private messages or allege violations without evidence.

The Catechism of the Catholic Church describes prudence as discerning suitable means (§1806) and fortitude as firmness in difficulty (§1808). These are Catholic moral teachings, not behavioral findings. Asch's controlled line-judgment study (1956, pp. 1–70) offers limited context about majority influence; it does not predict how Filipino students will respond in real ethical conflicts.

Scope and sources

This project uses close reading and documentary comparison, not interviews, surveys, or observations. It cannot say how often this occurs at UMak or prescribe one response for every group. The assigned theological texts are applied by analogy and retain their original contexts; other sources have distinct limits.
